Part I · Three Encounters Across a Lifetime

Ahab Died in the Process; the Reader Outlasted the Book

I stalked Moby-Dick, the book, like Ahab stalked the White Whale. The difference is that Ahab died in the process, and I feel, as I write this, still alive. At the end of the book, Moby-Dick continued to roam the seas.

Google Search coaches a reader that Herman Melville’s 1851 epic can be finished in “15 to 20 hours,” or “about 2 to 3 weeks, if you read an hour a day.” In practice, signing on for Moby-Dick felt less like reading a novel than shipping out on an ocean voyage stuck in the Strait of Hormuz during war. It took three separate seasons of life—and three entirely different reading mediums—before the book finally opened.

Part II · What Bogs the Reader Down

Sodden Sailors, Whale Brains, and the Minutiae of 135 Chapters

When I was a first-year undergraduate, Moby-Dick bogged me down with its nautical and whaling terms. At times, the plot drifted so slowly through the eddies of Melville’s sprawling prose that I felt trapped in a skid-row bar listening to a sodden old sailor gibble-gabble about the sacred lore of knot-tying, the ravages of scurvy, the benefits of owning a good pair of boots, scrimshawing, or the symptoms of gonorrhea. Once you are in, you can’t get out.

High school had drilled us to mine Silas Marner, The Scarlet Letter, and Lord of the Flies for “complex themes,” “dense, layered metaphors,” and hidden biblical allusions—all jibber-jabber to a teen brain sorting books before the lunch bell. On top of that, Melville refuses directness and turns encyclopedic whenever a whale crosses his path.

Part III · Beneath the Salt Spray

Your Well-Being Is Not Your Own—It Is a Monkey-Rope

Herman Melville is often misremembered as a sea-adventurer who wrote about whales and sailors. Strip away the brine and the blubber, however, and you find a philosopher obsessed with one terrifying truth: your well-being is never your own. It is loaned to you by strangers, held in precarious trust, and can be recalled without warning.

For Melville, interdependence is not the warm embrace of human solidarity—it is a monkey-rope, binding us to people we barely understand in a world that offers no guarantee of rescue. Without that tether, we are “isolatoes,” isolated fragments floating in an indifferent void. Yet with it, a stranger’s slip or a captain’s madness can drag the entire deck beneath the Pacific.

Part IV · The Closet Folder & Your Verdict

The Tragedy and the Miracle Are the Same

In my thirties, reading a thick annotated edition in fits and starts between fatherhood and teaching, I scribbled notes into a folder that sat hidden in a closet for decades—my mother’s warnings about bad penmanship proved right when I finally unfolded them. By seventy-nine, listening to twenty-three hours of prose through AirPods, I no longer needed to hunt down footnotes or conquer a vocabulary quiz. I just listened to the music of a voice that asks us to tremble before how completely we belong to one another.

“Your well-being is not your own—it’s loaned to you by others, and they can recall that loan at any moment. That’s what makes life both precious and petrifying.”
